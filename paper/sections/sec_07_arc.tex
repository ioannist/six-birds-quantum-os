\section{Results III: a registered failure, diagnosed and repaired inside the framework}
\label{sec:arc}

This section is the centerpiece of the paper, and Figure~\ref{fig:cycle} is its map. It starts from a registered failure (E2). The failure is diagnosed with the framework's own exact machinery, and a corrected statistic is re-registered (E7) together with a prediction that it would \emph{still lose} to the baseline on the original test. The correction is then carried to circuit-level noise (E8) and to closed-loop decoder control (E9). Throughout, E2's committed verdicts stand untouched; everything here is additive.

\subsection{The failure (E2)}
\label{sec:e2}

E2 tested drift detection on SURF(3). The decoder's declared model is N2 with $p = 3/100$. The true model is N3: the same noise plus an unmodeled correlated $ZZ$ error with $q = 1/50$ on the frozen qubit pair $(0,3)$. Two witnesses were compared with a baseline that monitors the decoder's logical failure rate. W1 is an \emph{oracle} witness that reads the true logical values in simulation, labeled non-deployable from the start. W2 is the syndrome-only witness, the one that would matter in practice. A witness ``detects'' at sample size $N$ when it raises an alarm in at least 9 of 10 seeds. Results (\gMeasured): W1 detected at $N = 500$ shots against the baseline's $1000$, a real factor of 2 but short of the registered factor of 4; W2 \emph{never reached the detection criterion within the frozen grid} (up to $N = 16000$; at most 5 of 10 seeds raised an alarm at any $N$). Both registered predictions failed (\gRegNeg). % F-026, F-005 (C-24)
The null calibration passed: false-positive rates were 0.0 for W1 and 0.02 for W2 against the registered 2\% bar. These are empirical rates over 50 seeded null runs; they estimate, rather than guarantee, the population false-alarm rate. % F-027, F-112

\subsection{The diagnosis: exact inputs, and mostly about the test itself}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

Before any correction was written, we analyzed the failure with the same moment engine the audits are built on. The mean shifts, covariance blocks and syndrome distributions below are exact rational objects; the scalar energies and divergences computed from them are floating-point evaluations of those exact inputs. Each of the four findings was then pinned as a test in the repository. The reflexive point we draw from findings 1 and 2 is an analogy, marked as such (\gInterp): the moment machinery that audits a schedule's coverage of the logical observables was turned on the witness itself, to ask how much of the drift signal it kept.

\paragraph{Finding 1: the statistic discards its mean signal (\gExact).} W2 was built from \emph{centered} covariance blocks, so the drift's direct effect on the mean entered only through its second-order footprint, never as a mean feature. The exact mean shift caused by the frozen drift is zero on seven of the eight checks and $-331776/9765625 \approx -0.0340$ on the eighth, with total energy $\|\Delta\mathrm{mean}_L\|^{2} = 0.0011542$ (a floating-point sum of the exact entries). % F-075 (C-25)
A real, exactly quantified direct signal was being left out.

\paragraph{Finding 2: a lift built for the wrong objective compresses the signal about $73\times$.} W2 mapped the shift in the syndrome covariance through the coverage-optimal map $A^{*}$. That map is fitted to predict \emph{logical values}, not to preserve \emph{sensitivity to drift}. The drift signal's energy is $\|\Delta K_{LL}\|_F^{2} = 0.0032632$ before the map and $4.4703 \times 10^{-5}$ after it (floating-point norms of exact covariance differences). % F-076 (C-26)
The compression ratio of about $73\times$ is our reading of those two numbers (\gInterp).

\paragraph{Finding 3: a borrowed null (\gInterp, confirmed by measurement below).} W2's alarm threshold was calibrated on W1's statistic, a differently scaled object, rather than on W2's own null distribution. % F-077 (C-27)

\paragraph{Finding 4: the frozen scenario is structurally hard for syndrome-only detection.} This is the key step. Means of XOR-combinations of checks are the Walsh--Hadamard coefficients of the syndrome distribution (Section~\ref{sec:domain}), so the full $2^{8}$-outcome syndrome distribution is exactly recoverable under both the declared and the true model (\gExact). From these two exact distributions we evaluate, numerically, the per-shot Kullback--Leibler divergence $D(\mathrm{true}\,\|\,\mathrm{declared})$ of the syndrome, in nats per shot. It is a per-shot measure of the evidence each shot carries about the drift. By Stein's lemma~\cite{cover2006elements}, it is the best achievable error exponent of an asymmetric test that holds fixed the probability of rejecting the drifted model when the drift is present; holding the false-alarm rate fixed instead calls for the reverse divergence. We use it as an information measure, not as a latency prediction. We compare it with the per-shot divergence of the logical-failure indicator, measured from $N = 2 \times 10^{6}$ shots per scenario (\gMeasured), for three placements of the injected error: % F-078 (C-28a, C-28b)

Our structural reading (\gInterp): drift that feeds a minimum-weight failure path is exactly the drift that the failure channel is built to notice and that syndrome decoding is built to correct silently. Of the three scenarios, the E2 pair puts a syndrome-only witness at the greatest disadvantage relative to failure-rate monitoring. This is a fact about this grid of scenarios, not a general worst-case statement, and it holds independently of findings 1--3. It shaped the re-registration: the corrected witness had to be tested across the \emph{range} that finding 4 exposes, with an honest prediction attached to each regime.

\subsection{The corrected witness, re-registered with a predicted failure (E7)}
\label{sec:e7}

The correction addresses findings 1 and 2 with one object. Take the vector of means of the degree-$\le 2$ probe family: all 8 native checks plus all 28 pairwise XOR products, that is, 36 exact Walsh--Hadamard coefficients. Use it \emph{directly}, with no centering and no lift. The corrected detection statistic is the quadratic form
\begin{equation}
T \;=\; (\hat\theta - \theta_{\mathrm{model}})^{\top}\, \Sigma_{\theta}^{+}\, (\hat\theta - \theta_{\mathrm{model}}),
\label{eq:w2b}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\theta$ is the empirical mean over $N$ shots, and the reference mean $\theta_{\mathrm{model}}$ and estimator covariance $\Sigma_\theta$ are exact under the declared model. It is a model-referenced Mahalanobis form of Hotelling type~\cite{hotelling1931ratio}; unlike the classical $T^{2}$, it uses the declared model's covariance rather than a sample estimate, and with the pseudoinverse it is the square of a seminorm. We claim no chi-square null distribution or power theorem for it. Instead, its alarm threshold is calibrated on its own null by a seeded parametric bootstrap, that is, Monte Carlo draws from the declared model~\cite{efron1979bootstrap}.

Around $T$ we registered a ladder of four rungs. W2a is the \emph{old} statistic with only its null recalibrated, which isolates finding 3. W2b is the quadratic statistic of Eq.~\eqref{eq:w2b}. W2c adds a matched-filter \emph{naming} step: it whitens the deviation, matches it against a dictionary of nine exact per-qubit drift signatures, and maps the best match forward to a logical direction. W2d wraps the statistic in a CUSUM sequential test~\cite{page1954continuous}, and the baseline receives the \emph{same} sequential upgrade. That removes one asymmetry between the two sides of the test design; it does not equalize their operating characteristics, and the null-rate checks below report those separately. Seven predictions were frozen from finding 4's numbers before implementation. Three outcomes carry the section (Figure~\ref{fig:e7}).

\paragraph{The off-support scenario: a large registered win.} On pair $(4,8)$, where Table~\ref{tab:kl} put about $773\times$ more per-shot information in the syndrome, the corrected rungs detect at $N = 1000$ (W2b and W2c) and $N = 2000$ (W2d). Neither baseline reaches the detection criterion anywhere in the frozen grid up to $N = 16000$. The registered bar asked for at least a $10\times$ advantage, and it held (\gRegPos). % F-078, F-080 (C-28b, C-30)

\paragraph{The original scenario: the predicted failure held.} Backed by Table~\ref{tab:kl}'s ratio of $0.665$, we registered in advance that on the original E2 pair $(0,3)$ \emph{no corrected rung would beat the baseline}. That is what happened: the baseline detects at $N = 1000$, the best corrected rungs at $N = 4000$ (\gRegPos, a predicted negative that held). % F-081 (C-31)
We give this result more weight than the win above, deliberately. A correction developed after a failure invites the suspicion of post-hoc storytelling: a statistic quietly tuned until the old test passes, the degenerating move in Lakatos's sense~\cite{lakatos1978methodology}. A frozen prediction, backed by exact computation, that the correction would \emph{still lose} on the original test, followed by exactly that loss, is the strongest evidence this arc can offer that the diagnosis explained the failure rather than fitting it. The near-parity pair $(2,5)$ was registered as descriptive, with no bar. It landed where its ratio of $1.100$ suggested: baseline at $1000$, W2b and W2c at $2000$, W2d at $8000$ (\gMeasured). % F-082

\paragraph{What failed.} Three E7 predictions failed (\gRegNeg{} each). First, the recalibrated-null prediction P7.1: we predicted that the old statistic's own-null threshold would be at most 10\% of the borrowed one, and measured a ratio of $1.33$. % F-079 (C-29)
Our reading (\gInterp): the reasoning behind the prediction compared the \emph{injected signal's} scale, of order $p$ against $p^2$, rather than the scale of \emph{null fluctuations} that a threshold actually calibrates.
Second, naming split (Figure~\ref{fig:naming}). Only 3 of 10 detecting seeds named a qubit in the true injection pair, against a bar of 8 of 10, while the forward-mapped overlap with the true logical direction was $0.869$, above its $0.6$ bar (\gMeasured). % F-083 (C-32)
Our reading (\gInterp): the naming dictionary is a signed matched filter built from finite differences of the exact signatures, and the overlap is taken in the two-dimensional space of the selected logical observables. The nine per-qubit signatures map to few distinct directions there, so a high overlap is a coarse check: it can pass while qubit-level naming fails, and it does not by itself localize the drift physically.
Third, the headline quadratic rung W2b had a null false-positive rate of 6\% against the 2\% bar. The other separately calibrated statistics, W2a and W2d, and both baselines passed; the naming rung W2c detects with W2b's statistic and threshold, so it shares W2b's calibration. % F-084 (C-32)

\subsection{Generalization to circuit-level noise (E8)}
\label{sec:e8}

The corrected statistic carries over directly to circuit-level noise. Detectors replace checks, and the degree-$\le 2$ family becomes the detectors plus their pairwise XOR products: 40 detectors and 820 features for the rotated distance-3 Stim memory circuit used. % F-088
Exact Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb{F}_2$ on the declared detector error patterns gives rank 40 of 40, so for this positive-noise model the detectors span their full space. % F-177
There is no exact moment engine at circuit level, so $\theta_{\mathrm{model}}$ and $\Sigma_\theta$ are estimated from a large calibration sample of the declared model ($2 \times 10^{5}$ shots), and the predictions were frozen from a disjoint-seed pilot before the scored run (Section~\ref{sec:methods-registration}). % F-089
The scored drift is a global doubling of the error rates. The baseline is the logical failure rate of a PyMatching decoder, under the same detection criterion.

The witness detects at $N_{\mathrm{det}} = 50$ against the baseline's $100$ (Figure~\ref{fig:e8}). This clears the pilot-frozen $2\times$ bar exactly. On the discrete grid $\{10, 20, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 2000\}$ that is the narrowest margin the grid can resolve, and the result should be read with that caveat attached, not as a comfortable win (\gRegPos, margin caveat mandatory). % F-086 (C-33)
Null false-positive rates were 0\% for both sides (\gRegPos). % F-087
One further detail is preserved in the deviations log: the pilot had suggested an advantage of about $5\times$, and the scored run delivered $2\times$. The pilot number is a smaller-sample estimate and is not the headline. % F-167, F-086

\subsection{Closing the loop: witness-triggered decoder recalibration (E9)}
\label{sec:e9}

The last experiment asks whether the witness's information is worth anything \emph{operationally}. Suppose the drifting channel is known by construction and the only question is \emph{when} to recalibrate. Does recalibrating when the witness fires buy logical fidelity per unit of recalibration budget?

\paragraph{Setup.} Time is divided into 40 epochs of 300 shots each, on a distance-3, 9-round memory circuit (72 detectors, 2628 degree-$\le 2$ features; exact $\mathbb{F}_2$ rank 72 of 72). % F-090, F-177
From epoch 15 on, one measurement-flip channel is elevated $20\times$. We chose a drift of a single channel because, in the pilot, uniformly rescaling all error rates changed the matching decoder's predictions only very slightly, which would leave a recalibration nothing to show. This is an empirical choice, not an invariance theorem. Uniform rescaling does change the matching weights, and not in proportion: doubling every rate of the distance-3 memory circuits from $0.005$ to $0.01$ (E8's drift) multiplies individual edge weights by factors between about $0.76$ and $0.89$. % F-105, F-176
Recalibrating means matching the observed mean detector-flip rate to a simulated curve of candidate values for the drifting channel, then rebuilding the decoder from the chosen candidate's detector error model. Five decoder policies run over 10 seeds:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \emph{static}: never recalibrate (the floor);
\item \emph{oracle}: switch to the decoder built for the true model at the true drift epoch, without being charged a recalibration event;
\item \emph{budget-matched blind schedule}: recalibrate at uniformly random epochs;
\item \emph{frequent blind schedule}: recalibrate every fifth epoch, 8 times per seed;
\item \emph{witness-triggered}: recalibrate when the quadratic witness, evaluated on each epoch's detector batch against a calibrated threshold, fires; then keep monitoring against the recalibrated reference, which corrects false alarms.
\end{itemize}
Two scope points follow from the design. The witness policy works on whole epochs: when the witness fires on a completed epoch's detector data, the recalibrated decoder decodes that same epoch. This is legitimate batch decoding with one epoch of latency. The candidate value is chosen from detector statistics, never from logical outcomes. The oracle is a model-known minimum-weight-matching comparator; it is not a proof of an optimal decoder, and it does not bound the measured error rates of other policies pointwise. % C-34, C-39

\paragraph{How the budget was matched.} Experiments like this are easiest to rig by accident at the budget-matching step, so we describe it exactly. On each seed, the matched blind schedule recalibrates at exactly as many epochs as the witness policy did on that seed, chosen uniformly at random on an independently generated timeline. The realized counts are $[1,1,2,2,1,1,3,2,1,2]$ for both policies, mean 1.6. % F-093
The match is therefore exact in recalibration events and is fixed retrospectively, after the witness has run; it is an offline counterfactual and does not match every computational or calibration cost. The frozen run was first executed with a fixed-period schedule aimed at one event per run, while the witness policy's realized mean was 1.6. Internal review caught the mismatch, the schedule was changed to exact per-seed matching, and the experiment was re-run and re-frozen with the prediction bars unchanged (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).

\paragraph{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:e9} shows the mean post-drift logical error rate per epoch, averaged over seeds.
\begin{itemize}
\item Witness-triggered: $0.0930$, about $0.0014$ above the oracle's $0.0916$ (gap $0.00143$ to three significant figures). The frozen bar was a gap of at most $0.01$ (\gRegPos). % F-091 (C-34; rounding authorized in claims.md)
\item Against the budget-matched blind schedule: $0.0930$ vs.\ $0.1115$. At identical per-seed budgets the witness policy is lower by $0.0185$ (\gRegPos). % F-092, F-093 (C-34)
Seed by seed, it is lower on 9 of 10 seeds, and the paired standard error of the mean difference is $0.0035$ (\gMeasured). % F-175
\item The static floor sits at $0.1185$. % F-097
\item The frequent schedule spends 8 events per seed, five times the witness policy's mean, and scores $0.0946$. Its deficit of $0.0016$ to the witness policy is smaller than the paired standard error of $0.0020$ at 10 seeds, so this comparison is unresolved (\gMeasured, no bar). % F-094, F-095, F-175 (C-35)
\item Pre-drift trigger rates, descriptive: on the scored seeds the witness never triggered before the drift, while the matched and frequent schedules recalibrated before the drift on 50\% and 100\% of seeds. % F-096
\end{itemize}

Two scope statements bound this result. First, the resolved comparison is \emph{witness against budget-matched blind scheduling}. Whether the witness beats \emph{frequent} blind scheduling is unresolved at this sample size; spending five times the budget blindly closes most, but not all, of the gap. % C-35
Second, the recalibration searches only the one channel known by construction to drift. E9 therefore demonstrates the value of the witness for \emph{timing and budget}, not for drift \emph{identification}, which is E7's naming step and was tested separately. % C-39 (F-105)
