\section{Results I: certificates on ground truth}
\label{sec:results-certificates}

This section reports the four audits on declared, exactly known models. This is where the framework's claims are sharpest, because everything it certifies can be checked against ground truth. Experiments are labeled E1--E6 as in the repository. Registered verdicts appear here whether they passed or failed; Section~\ref{sec:negatives} then looks at the failures as a group.

\subsection{Coverage: the residual computes the right object (E1, E3)}
\label{sec:results-coverage}

\paragraph{Theorem checks.} Two theorems of the framework were verified on every model where they apply (\gTheorem). The chain-rule identity $\Xi(D \mid L \cup M) = \Xires - \mathrm{discharge}(M \mid L, D)$ holds with a discrepancy of exactly zero in rational arithmetic on the tested models, and $\delta \le \varepsilon$ holds on every model built. Section~\ref{sec:repro} names the tests that check them. % C-06, C-07, F-162

\paragraph{Residuals and ablations.} With every check scheduled, the residual traces are $\operatorname{tr}\Xi = 0.0837$ for REP(3), $0.0740$ for REP(5) and $0.1754$ for SURF(3) (\gExact). % F-041 (C-08)
Dropping any single check strictly increases the residual, and on REP(5) dropping an end check differs from dropping a middle one, as registered (\gRegPos). % F-002 (C-09)
On average an end-check drop costs more: the mean residual increase is $0.0593$ for end checks and $0.0204$ for middle checks (\gExact). % F-042 (C-09)
Duplicated checks show exactly zero discharge, both in E1 and in E3's candidate form (\gRegPos). The saturation null holds exactly, as the theory requires. % F-004, F-011 (C-12)

\paragraph{The cleanest result: the degree ladder reaches the exact optimum.} Take REP(3) under bit-flip noise and extend its two native checks to the complete degree-2 family by adding their product. The residual trace drops from $0.083680$ to $0.027575$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}). The second number equals, exactly, the minimum mean-squared error with which \emph{any} function of the syndrome can estimate the logical $\pm1$ observable, which we obtain independently by enumerating the error distribution. Both are the rational number $47291/1715000$, and the experiment confirms their equality as an equality of fractions (\gExact). % F-046, F-173 (C-10)
The registered prediction that the top rung equals the exact minimum mean-squared error therefore held (\gRegPos). % F-010 (C-10)
The equality is exact for a simple reason. REP(3) has four possible syndromes, and the intercept, the two syndrome bits and their product span every function on four points. The best affine estimator over that family is therefore the best estimator of any kind, namely the conditional mean. Note what this quantity is: a squared-error estimate of the logical observable, not the success probability of a decoder that must output one bit. This is our sharpest instance of ``the audit computes the right object'': a coverage certificate derived from SBT's chain rule lands, digit for digit as a fraction, on a quantity a QEC practitioner would compute by brute force. We use the word ``optimal'' for this result because it attains the enumerated optimum. Elsewhere in the paper the word appears only in the standard names of mathematical objects, such as the coverage-optimal map $A^{*}$, or as a by-construction property of the first greedy step, never as a performance claim.

\paragraph{Greedy selection matches or beats the tested orders at every budget.} On SURF(3), greedy check selection by the chain rule lowers the residual from $0.4345$ with no checks to $0.1754$ with all of them. At \emph{every} intermediate budget its residual is at or below that of every tested baseline: lexicographic order and 10 seeded random orders (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}; \gRegPos). % F-044, F-045, F-009 (C-11)
This is a registered comparison against those baselines, not a general optimality statement. Only the first greedy step is optimal by construction. Greedy selection carries the classical $(1-1/e)$ guarantee only for submodular objectives~\cite{nemhauser1978greedy,krause2008sensor}, and variance reduction by a set of regressors is not submodular in general~\cite{das2011submodular}, so we claim no such guarantee. Given a candidate list padded with duplicates, the greedy selector also stops before exhausting it rather than spending budget on checks that provably add nothing (\gRegPos). % F-012

\subsection{Existence: the certificate does classification work (E4)}
\label{sec:results-existence}

The typed existence certificate was computed on ground-truth Markov models of the error-correction cycle. For the REP(3) packages under bit-flip noise in Table~\ref{tab:certificate}, its idempotence defect, retention error and multiplicity are exact rational computations (\gExact); closure deficits, and the certificates of the hidden-state models below, are evaluated numerically. The baseline package is REP(3) under bit-flip noise with the standard decoder. It certifies, with $\delta = 0.007144875$, $\varepsilon = 0.00725$ (so the theorem bound $\delta \le \varepsilon$ is nearly tight), multiplicity 2, and status \texttt{certified}. % F-048 (C-13)
A deliberately broken decoder that maps everything to one logical state is caught by the multiplicity guard. It has $\delta = 0$ \emph{exactly}, perfect idempotence, but multiplicity 1, so its status is \texttt{trivialized} (\gRegPos{} for the guard). % F-049, F-016 (C-13, C-14)
The pair captures the certificate idea in one contrast. A small idempotence defect alone would have certified the broken decoder; the typed certificate refuses it.

Sweeping the physical error rate shows the defect growing (\gExact): $\delta = 0.000298$, $0.007145$, $0.026432$ and $0.082368$ at $p = 1/100$, $1/20$, $1/10$ and $1/5$. The sweep labels a package \texttt{degrading} once $\delta$ exceeds $0.05$, which first happens at $p = 1/5$. % F-050 (C-13)
That label uses the defect alone. The full classifier would return \texttt{trivialized} at $p = 1/5$, because no prototype is stable there (multiplicity $0$, $\varepsilon = 0.104$) and multiplicity takes priority over the defect threshold (\gExact). % F-166 (C-13)
Table~\ref{tab:certificate} collects the classification.

Two further components behaved as designed on hidden-state models. First, the closure deficit tracks an out-of-sample predictability proxy $\Delta_{\mathrm{pred}}$: the held-out improvement in next-label log-likelihood when a predictor of the macro label sees the previous label as well as the current one. Across the declared table of models and horizons, the Pearson correlation is $r = 0.936$ with the latching model N5 included (\gMeasured). % F-054 (C-15)
The registered bar $r \ge 0.9$ held (\gRegPos). % F-017 (C-15)
Two qualifications matter. Without N5 the correlation is $r = -0.171$, so the registered correlation is carried by N5 and does not hold on the remaining models. % F-054 (C-15)
And the proxy is not the closure deficit itself. It measures a one-step macro-history gain in a long simulated run, started from the stationary law for nonabsorbing models and from the declared finite-horizon weighting for N5; it repeats the same value across horizons for a given model, and for N5 the long run can wash out the finite-horizon law that defines $CD_\tau$; the coefficient describes the declared table, not a sample of independent models. Second, the route-mismatch diagnostic shows the intended leakage signature on N5: the decoded label stays channel-accurate ($0.75$) while $RM$ grows severalfold with the horizon, peaking at $7.28\times$ the baseline. It saturates below the frozen $10\times$ bar, a \gRegNeg{} discussed in Section~\ref{sec:negatives}. % F-052 (P4.3)

\subsection{Memory: exact witnesses, a measured payoff, and a control that overturned our hypothesis (E5)}
\label{sec:results-memory}

\paragraph{Witnesses separate the noise models exactly.} The predictive-quotient audit was run on REP(3) packages under three noise models (\gExact). Under memoryless noise (N1) it finds \emph{no} memory witnesses: $|Q| = |M| = 4$ and $\Delta^{\max} = 0$. Under hidden-mode noise (N4) it finds 4 witnesses, MaxFiber 2, and $\Delta^{\max} = 112329952/1318359375 \approx 0.0852$. Under latching noise (N5) it finds 4 witnesses, MaxFiber 2, $|M| = 8$ predictive classes over $|Q| = 4$ current classes, and $\Delta^{\max} = 539/1250 = 0.4312$. % F-028, F-029, F-030 (C-16)
Every package exports a well-defined map from predictive to current classes, confirming that $M$ refines $Q$ (\gExact). % F-171 (C-16)
These are certificates in the strict sense. Within the declared catalog of histories, events and continuations, they say exactly which currently indistinguishable histories assign different probabilities to some declared future event (on N4 and N5), and that no such pair exists on N1. Any predictor that uses only current information must therefore err by at least $\Delta^{\max}/2$ on some declared event: at least $0.0426$ on N4 and $539/2500 = 0.2156$ on N5 (\gExact). % F-170 (C-16)
Whether a decoder can turn this difference into performance is the separate, measured question below.

\paragraph{Currentization names the hidden coordinate.} Adding the mode bit as an observable dissolves every witness with a single added observable; no set of pure check bits does (\gRegPos). % F-021 (C-17)
The audit therefore does more than detect hidden memory. It identifies \emph{which} added observable would expose it.

\paragraph{The protocol trap overturned our hypothesis.} We built a two-phase alternating noise schedule as the intended ``schedule artifact.'' We registered the prediction that internalizing the schedule (adding the phase as an autonomous state coordinate) would dissolve its witnesses and classify it \texttt{artifact\_trap}. That prediction \emph{failed}: internalization preserved all 4 witnesses, and the package classifies as \texttt{genuine\_memory\_after\_internalization} (\gExact{} for the classification). % F-032, F-070 (C-20)
The registered artifact hypothesis therefore failed (\gRegNeg). % F-020 (C-20)
Our reading (\gInterp): a deterministic alternation between $p_0$ and $3p_0$ carries real predictive content, because the current phase predicts next-round statistics, so the trap correctly declined to call it an artifact. This is easy to misreport, so we state it plainly. The control mechanism worked, and what it falsified was \emph{our} registered hypothesis. It is a negative result for that hypothesis, not a demonstration of the trap catching an artifact. % C-20

\paragraph{The minimal machine.} The registered decoder machine is a declared, quantized belief automaton with 8 nodes: four syndrome values times two levels of a coarsened belief about the hidden mode. It is a packaged approximation, not the exact filter of the hidden Markov model. Exact partition refinement finds the smallest automaton with the same next-syndrome predictions. It has 2 predictive states, one for each belief level, and only 1 of them is reachable from the frozen initial prototype (\gExact). % F-172 (C-20b)
At the frozen defaults the declared machine therefore never changes its prediction: its stored syndrome is redundant for this readout, and its belief coordinate never leaves its initial level. This matches the registered payoff result. The accuracy-scored comparison between the machine and a syndrome-only decoder came back exactly nil (P5.2, \gRegNeg). The reason is also exact. REP(3) sees only the bit-flip part of the noise, and with flip probabilities below $1/2$ in both modes the most likely correction is the same in every mode, so accuracy-scored decoders tie by construction. % F-069, F-033

\paragraph{Memory's measured value, under a proper scoring rule.} Accuracy cannot see the value of memory here, so a post-registration study, labeled as such and leaving every frozen verdict untouched, rescored next-round predictions by negative log-likelihood. This is a strictly proper scoring rule~\cite{gneiting2007proper}, under which better-calibrated probabilities earn credit even when the most likely outcome does not change. At each operating point, one 300{,}000-round trajectory of REP(3) under N4 is split in half. Anything that is fitted uses only the first half, and every predictor is scored on the same second half. All predictors read the full round outcome: both syndrome bits and the increment of the logical observable. They are therefore predictors of the declared signal, not syndrome-only decoders that could be deployed as such. Figure~\ref{fig:payoff} shows the results, as gains in nats per round over a static predictor that ignores history (\gMeasured). % F-034..F-039 (C-18, C-19)
\begin{itemize}
\item A \emph{mode oracle}, which is told the hidden mode, gains $0.0109$ at the frozen defaults and $0.0582$ at a declared louder operating point. % F-034, F-037
\item The \emph{Bayes filter}~\cite{rabiner1989hmm}, which knows the model and the initial mode but not the current one, gains $0.00190$ and $0.0365$. % F-034, F-037
\item A \emph{run-length machine} with $K$ states counts consecutive rounds on which one syndrome bit and the logical increment are both trivial (capped at $K-1$), and predicts from a table trained on the first half. It gains a positive amount at every $K$ tested. Its gain rises with $K$ at the frozen defaults ($0.00015$ at $K{=}2$ to $0.00091$ at $K{=}16$) and up to $K{=}8$ at the louder point, where $K{=}16$ sits slightly below $K{=}8$ ($0.01036$ vs.\ $0.01052$). % F-035, F-038 (C-18)
\item \emph{Belief rounding}, which snaps the filter's continuous belief to one of $K$ levels fitted on the first half at every step, loses value at small $K$. At the frozen defaults it is negative at every tested resolution ($-0.0136$, $-0.0044$, $-0.0052$, $-0.0044$ for $K = 2, 4, 8, 16$; worst at $K{=}2$ and not monotone in $K$). At the louder point it is negative up to $K{=}8$ and positive at $K{=}16$ ($+0.0281$). That positive value contradicted our stated expectation that rounding would lose value at every tested $K$, and we report it as such. % F-036, F-039 (C-19)
\end{itemize}
Relative to the mode oracle, the Bayes filter recovers about 17\% of the oracle's gain at the frozen defaults and about 63\% at the louder point, and the 2-state run-length machine recovers about 8\% and 12\% of the filter's gain (\gInterp). % F-040
The oracle's measured gains are finite-sample estimates; they lie within $0.0003$ of the analytic stationary information values ($0.0107$ and $0.0579$), which are a different quantity. % F-034, F-037
Our packaging-theoretic reading (\gInterp): summarizing a short run-length statistic of the observed record is cheap and safe, whereas quantizing an \emph{internal belief} has a resolution threshold below which it destroys value.

\subsection{Pricing: exact machinery, negative evidence (E6)}
\label{sec:results-pricing}

The pricing audit is this paper's clean full negative, and we give it the same prominence as the positives. The machinery is exact where declared and runs correctly. On REP(5), exhaustive enumeration gives the value curve $V(b)$ and the shadow prices $\lambda(b)$ as exact rationals (\gExact). % F-055, F-057, F-174 (C-21a)
The greedy selector comes within $0.00405$ of the exact curve at every budget (\gMeasured). % F-056 (C-21a)
Every registered \emph{prediction} about pricing behaviour failed at the frozen tolerances (\gRegNeg{} throughout): % C-21b
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item $\lambda$ was predicted to be nonincreasing after its maximum. Instead, $\lambda$ jumps back up at several budgets ($b = 3, 7, 10, 12, 15$), an irregular, nonmonotone pattern that comes from mixing degree-1 checks (cost 1) with degree-2 checks (cost 2). % F-022, F-071, F-057
\item A slack point $b^{*} < b_{\max}$ was predicted. Instead $b^{*} = b_{\max}$ for both code families (next section). % F-023, F-072
\item The consequence test (removing the last unit before $b^{*}$ should shift the sampled logical error rate detectably) did not separate: $\Delta = -0.000135$ against a combined standard error of $0.000119$. % F-059, F-073
\item Permuted proxy costs were strictly worse at only 36.9\% of budget points, against a registered bar of 80\%. % F-060, F-074
\end{itemize}
We do not soften this. As instantiated and thresholded here, the pricing primitive produced no positively evidenced result. Section~\ref{sec:negatives} suggests that the failures are errors of threshold scale and test design rather than refutations of the pricing concept (\gInterp), but on this prototype's evidence the primitive stands negative.
